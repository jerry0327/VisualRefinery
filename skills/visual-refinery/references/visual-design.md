# Visual Design Reference

Read this reference when an artifact needs substantive layout, typography, imagery, chart, or accessibility decisions.

The goal is not to impose one aesthetic. The goal is to make visual form serve the content.

## 1. Establish visual hierarchy

A reader should be able to distinguish at a glance:

1. the page's main conclusion or purpose;
2. the supporting evidence;
3. secondary explanation;
4. metadata, citations, and notes.

Use size, weight, position, spacing, contrast, and grouping deliberately. Do not rely on color alone.

Avoid pages where every box, number, and heading has equal visual weight.

## 2. Typography

Use fonts that are appropriate to the medium and likely to render reliably in the target environment.

General rules:

- use a small number of type styles;
- keep body text comfortably readable at real viewing size;
- maintain consistent heading levels;
- avoid over-tight line spacing;
- avoid very long line lengths in reports;
- keep captions, footnotes, labels, and citations readable rather than ornamental;
- protect extra space around text when font substitution is possible;
- avoid all-caps paragraphs and excessive tracking.

For presentation slides, body text should usually be large enough to read from the expected viewing distance. If the content only fits at tiny sizes, restructure it.

For reports and PDFs, optimize for sustained reading rather than screen-only density.

## 3. Grid, alignment, and spacing

Use a repeatable alignment system.

- align related edges;
- keep margins consistent;
- use spacing to express grouping;
- use columns only when they improve reading order;
- keep visual baselines stable across repeated page types;
- preserve enough outer margin for export and print tolerances.

Whitespace is useful when it creates hierarchy. It is not automatically good.

A large accidental empty lower half often signals that content was not edited or balanced. Fix it by merging, expanding useful analysis, introducing a meaningful visual, or rebalancing the layout—not by adding filler shapes.

## 4. Visual motifs and style

Choose a style that follows from the subject, audience, and distribution context.

A visual motif can create coherence, but keep it restrained. Examples include:

- a consistent image crop treatment;
- a repeated diagram language;
- a particular border or frame logic;
- a recurring annotation style;
- a consistent chart treatment;
- a clear light/dark page rhythm.

Do not confuse a motif with repetitive decoration. Repeating the same colored stripe, gradient blob, or oversized number on every page does not create meaningful design.

## 5. Color

Use color for hierarchy, grouping, state, and emphasis.

- choose a dominant neutral or background system;
- keep the number of semantic colors limited;
- reserve strong accents for information that truly needs attention;
- ensure contrast remains sufficient at normal viewing size;
- do not encode meaning by color alone when the distinction matters;
- consider grayscale or poor-projector performance when relevant.

Do not default to a generic corporate blue palette merely because the topic is professional.

## 6. Images and photography

Every significant image should answer a reader question.

Ask what the reader learns from seeing it that the text alone does not provide.

Prefer, when available:

- official or licensed subject-specific photography;
- real screenshots;
- original figures;
- clinical/scientific images with appropriate permission;
- maps;
- product or environment photography;
- diagrams that clarify structure or mechanism.

Avoid:

- watermarked images;
- low-resolution assets;
- distorted aspect ratios;
- unrelated stock photography;
- decorative AI imagery when real subject evidence is available;
- screenshots containing irrelevant browser chrome or private information.

Crop deliberately. Do not stretch.

## 7. Text over images

Never assume text is readable merely because it has a shadow.

Avoid placing text directly over bright skies, reflections, white walls, busy diagrams, strong highlights, or high-frequency backgrounds.

Use one or more of:

- an opaque or semi-opaque panel;
- a directional gradient;
- local darkening or blur;
- a solid caption band;
- relocation to a quieter image region.

Inspect the complete composition at normal viewing size afterward.

## 8. Charts and quantitative visuals

Use a chart only when the visual encoding makes the pattern easier to understand than a table or sentence.

Choose the encoding based on the question:

- bars for magnitude comparisons;
- lines for change over ordered time;
- scatterplots for relationships;
- dot plots for compact comparisons;
- small multiples for repeated comparable patterns;
- tables when exact lookup matters more than shape;
- flow or Sankey diagrams only when movement or allocation is the point.

General chart rules:

- state the conclusion in the chart title or nearby heading;
- label units and denominators;
- expose meaningful baselines;
- avoid unnecessary 3D effects;
- avoid rainbow palettes unless the variable is genuinely categorical and all categories matter;
- directly label important series when practical;
- keep legends close to the data;
- distinguish uncertainty from point estimates;
- avoid implying precision the data does not support.

Prefer native editable charts when the final format supports them.

## 9. Diagrams and process visuals

Use diagrams to make relationships visible.

A good diagram should reduce cognitive work. If the reader must decode decorative connectors, excessive icons, or arbitrary geometry, it has failed.

- keep directionality consistent;
- use clear node labels;
- make decision branches visually distinct;
- minimize crossing lines;
- use annotations to explain exceptions;
- align diagram complexity with the audience.

## 10. Tables

Tables are useful for exact comparison, criteria, specifications, or dense structured evidence.

- put the comparison dimension in the leftmost column when practical;
- highlight only the rows or cells that drive the decision;
- keep units in headers or values consistently;
- avoid tables that require microscopic type;
- split or redesign very wide tables;
- do not use alternating color bands if spacing and rules already provide sufficient grouping.

## 11. Accessibility

Consider accessibility as part of design, not a final patch.

Where relevant:

- use sufficient text/background contrast;
- do not rely on color alone;
- maintain readable minimum text sizes;
- preserve logical reading order;
- provide meaningful alt text or descriptions for essential visuals when the format supports it;
- avoid red/green-only comparisons;
- do not place critical information only in hover states or speaker notes;
- consider cognitive load, jargon density, and language proficiency.

Accessibility requirements vary by output format and audience. Apply the strongest practical standard the medium supports.

## 12. Citations and source credits

Citations should support the specific claim shown and remain readable.

Do not allow citation styling to dominate the page, but do not make it illegible to preserve aesthetics.

For images, maintain an asset manifest when provenance or licensing matters.

## 13. Anti-patterns

Avoid these recurring AI-artifact patterns unless the content genuinely demands them:

- identical rounded cards covering every page;
- oversized numbers with little interpretive context;
- generic gradient backgrounds;
- icon grids used instead of real explanation;
- fake “dashboard” gauges for non-dashboard information;
- four equally weighted quadrants regardless of content;
- decorative timelines with no meaningful temporal structure;
- random stock photos beside unrelated bullet lists;
- excessive badges, pills, and labels;
- tiny body type hidden inside attractive containers;
- a different visual style on every page;
- a single inflexible template applied to every topic.

The best visual system is one the reader notices mainly because the information is easy to understand.
